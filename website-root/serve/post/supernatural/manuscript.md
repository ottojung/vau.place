The windows sit low; from inside, passing traffic moves along the upper edge of the view like a slow mechanical ceiling. The tables keep the same measured distance from the walls, aisles just wide enough for a tray to pass without contact; the chairs face each other in pairs. The queue bends once before the till in an L-shape that keeps the doorway clear.

I take my place in it, moving forward each time the drawer opens with its tired ring. Above the counter, three menus are printed in two tight columns; when my turn comes I trust the digits more than the words and repeat one of the numbers, watching the cashier's hand go straight to the corresponding row as if confirming a mapping I had only guessed at. Tray in hand, I take a table by the wall.

A colleague on the municipal-systems contract that brought me here has given me the name of the man I am waiting for. He knows the local engineering scene, I am told, and which names on procurement papers correspond to people who can actually fix things.

There is a newspaper on the next chair. I pick it up. Liège has held a waffle competition; the winner is photographed behind an enormous plate of waffles, with three judges smiling beside her. I turn a few pages, looking for science. The laboratory says no one was at risk. The newspaper uses the word *escaped* three times. I fold it and put it back.

He arrives late and apologizes before sitting down. His tram, he says, underwent “a spontaneous adjustment to the route.” He pauses, then corrects himself.

“Not spontaneous. Sorry. A signal failed, or somebody changed the route. We just don't know which yet.”

We exchange the usual confirmations—my name, his, the fact that we have both found the correct café in the correct district on the correct day. He tells me he has worked elections—“not politics,” he says quickly, “interfaces.” As he talks, he keeps straightening small things: the sugar packet, the spoon. When conversation pauses, he taps the table as though confirming liveness.

I write down the useful parts and leave some of the commentary out.

The commentary gets harder to separate from the useful parts when he picks up one of the biscuits beside his coffee.

“You can tell a lot about a nation from how its biscuits break.”

I look at him. “How they break?”

“The English ones wait for tea and then break where you expect. The French fracture elegantly, usually along the line that leaves the prettier half. Belgian biscuits begin along one crack, reconsider, and finish by inventing a third.”

That seems ridiculous. I don't see what national character has to do with a biscuit, but he is already unwrapping it.

“See?”

I say nothing about the biscuit and ask how he came to work on elections.

The polling station was a school. There were folding tables, metal chairs, extension leads taped to the floor, stacks of forms, pencils attached to string, cardboard screens, a clock protected by a wire cage, two wastebaskets, a cabinet of children's paintings turned to face the wall, and a carpet whose pattern is an argument against democracy.

He had been sent there as technician, a word expansive enough to cover whatever had not yet been assigned to the others. On his table lay the checklist: confirm serial numbers, confirm counters at zero, confirm seals intact; note every confirmation in ink, sign where indicated. Two party representatives sat near the door. The bureau had a president, seconded for the day from the commune, whose signature would certify the count. She had signed returns in Schaerbeek for eleven years; in that district a signed return was the count. A schoolteacher seconded as a watcher stood where she could see both the machines and the clerks and seemed unsure which of them constituted the real work.

Voting itself went normally. He answered questions, checked displays, and waited for something to require him.

After the polls closed, the clerks began checking the totals. The work moved line by line until one of them stopped halfway through a candidate's result. She looked back at the list total, then at the candidate total, and read both again.

The candidate had more preferential votes than the entire list containing him.

She asked the clerk beside her to read the two figures. He did. They exchanged places at the display and read them again.

“Check this one,” somebody said.

They checked the machine identifier, the register, the media from which the totals had been read, and then the totals again. They assumed a check had been missed and went through the list again.

The air in the school had become very dry. Paper rasped when the clerks separated sheets, and once a wool sleeve snapped against the back of a plastic chair.

For a while nobody called it an *error*. That word would have started another procedure: forms, witnesses, preserved media, work frozen where it stood. So they talked around it. *This one*. *These two figures*. *Check it again.*

The same numbers survived every obvious check. The contradiction could now be written plainly:

$$
\boxed{\text{candidate preferences } \leq \text{ party list total } \leq \text{ district total}}
$$

The president asked the technician, quietly, whether she could sign. He understood the question: not whether the number was right, but whether the machine could be vouched for. He said he could not vouch for it. She held her pen over the box where the count would go. The figure the machine had printed would certify a contradiction; any other figure would alter the record; an empty box would fail to certify the district. She wrote the machine's figure, drew one line through it, wrote the corrected figure beside it, and initialled the correction. Then she asked the two representatives to witness it. One did. The other looked at the struck figure for a long time, and put down his pen without signing.

Eventually somebody used the word *error*, and the formal investigation began.

The technician checked the machine and the media again. The schoolteacher tried to be helpful by pointing out each time the computer made a small sound, as if identifying birds.

The woman with the adding tape asked if lightning could do it. “Not tonight,” the technician said. “Tonight is dry.”

They made a ledger of what would be convenient to blame and crossed each item off.

- ~~a transposed digit~~
- ~~a repeated line~~
- ~~a wrong candidate mapping~~
- ~~a duplicate import~~
- ~~a machine identifier copied from the next station~~
- ~~a power interruption~~
- ~~lightning~~

Each explanation had some trace it ought to have left. None of the expected traces was there.

When the figures survived another pass, someone subtracted one total from the other and wrote the difference in the margin: 4 096. The technician recognized the shape at once:

\[
4096 = 2^{12}.
\]

A difference of exactly one power of two made a bit flip an obvious suspect.

A physicist was eventually pressed into service because she happened to live nearby. She arrived by bicycle, helmet still in hand. By then nobody needed her to notice \(2^{12}\); what they wanted was a reason for one bit to change.

She made them go through the ordinary possibilities again. Bad input, bad arithmetic, bad code: each had somewhere to leave a trace. When those routes led back to the same number, the question shifted to the memory itself. A memory cell can be disturbed physically, without the program asking it to change. An energetic particle passing through the semiconductor can deposit enough charge to alter the bit it crosses. If that bit was the position worth 4 096, a zero becoming one would add exactly 4 096 to the stored count.

They wrote *single-event upset* on the list of possible causes.

The technician objected that bits did not change for no reason. The physicist agreed. A particle crossing a memory cell was a reason; the difficulty was that the reason would already be gone.

Later, the machine was tested, the software examined, and the result reconstructed. No software defect was found that explained the discrepancy. The experts' report concluded that it could *very probably* be attributed to a spontaneous and random inversion of a binary position in the computer's working memory. The report did not identify the particle or establish what had disturbed the memory.

Later retellings usually supplied ionizing radiation, and newspapers condensed that into a *cosmic flea bite*. By the time the incident reached programmers, the whole thing was often filed under *computer error*. That label did not tell the clerks what they could safely sign. What stayed with me was the qualification in the report: *very probably*.

By the time he finishes, the cafeteria has thinned and our coffee has gone cold. He straightens the sugar packet, which does not need straightening, and does not look up.

He believed the next count should be checked in more than one way, with records that the machine could not quietly change. I agreed with him.

I did not write a field note that afternoon. Years later, when I began the dossier, I put this under his story:

**Field Note #1. I checked the totals again after I read the report. They had not changed.**

There is an older photograph I keep in a folder called *Proofs I Do Not Argue With*. In it, a moth lies taped to a page of an engineering log.

The page belongs to the Harvard Mark II. On 9 September 1947, its operators found a moth in Relay #70, Panel F, taped it into the log, and wrote beside it: “First actual case of bug being found.” The cause had been found and kept with the report. I used to find that photograph reassuring. ([National Museum of American History][1])

**Somewhere between midnight and the first ferry.**

The trouble was an order that would neither finish nor fail. A customer could press *Pay*, and sometimes the answer would not come. I could not safely run the request again: the first attempt might already have taken the money. The company's office was empty, so I went in alone.

The building backed onto the harbour. A strip of quay light crossed the carpet; beyond it the desks were dark. In the server room the fans kept their usual pitch. I had worked nights before. Without other people there, I could hear the fans clearly enough to notice a change in their pitch.

At 00:41 I caught the failure. The application stopped at the same line it had stopped at earlier, waiting for the database to answer. I attached a tracer that had broken better men than me and waited for the next stall. The next run succeeded. Then the next. I removed the tracer, and within minutes the process hung again.

A familiar kind of nuisance. Watching a program changes its timing; anyone who has debugged a race knows that. I wrote *timing* at the top of a fresh page. I was relieved to have a word for it.

I introduced print statements as if soothing a friend — *tell me what you are thinking when you do this*.

With the extra lines the application ran perfectly. Without them it stopped. I removed them a second time, watched the cursor blink, and saw the stall return.

The next experiment was even smaller. I left the code alone and opened a monitor on the other screen. For an hour there was no failure. I closed the monitor. The request stalled on the very next attempt.

I checked the clock. I checked that I had in fact closed the window. I reopened it; the stalled request completed before I touched anything else. That, too, was possible. A delayed response can arrive at any moment. I wrote the time down to the second.

The room had a glass partition, and my monitor cast a reflection in it. Once, reaching to turn on another tool, I caught sight of a pale movement beside my reflected shoulder. It was my own hand. I kept my eyes on the glass until I was sure.

At two I repeated the experiment. At three I changed the test so that it would run without a human operator. It failed twice while I was away making coffee. Both times the logs showed an ordinary wait, and both times the process had recovered before I returned. When I stayed to watch, it ran without complaint.

By then I had begun to dislike successful tests.

I knew what this sounded like. I had been working too long, and I had begun arranging accidents into a story about myself. A debugger perturbs the system. Coffee breaks change the load. The failures were real; the impression that something was choosing when to fail was mine.

I said this aloud. Hearing my voice in the empty office made the explanation feel less persuasive, which was another effect for which I could supply an ordinary cause.

At 04:12 I turned the main monitor away from me and left only the corner of its screen visible in the glass. I meant to see whether the request would fail without the bright window in front of my face. It did. On the reflected screen a small red mark appeared beside the waiting transaction. I turned around at once.

There was no red mark. There was no waiting transaction. The system had completed, and the log gave an ordinary completion time, and I sat trying to remember whether I had seen the colour or only expected it, a difference that should have been obvious and would not settle, while the heating came on and the pipes began to click overhead and a chair creaked somewhere beyond the partition, and I found that I was listening between the clicks for a sound that would not belong to the building.

At 04:56 the request stopped again. Everything I needed was on the screen, the tracer one command away, and I sat with my hands above the keys and did not type it, not because I thought the trace would show nothing but because the idea had come, sudden and complete, that attaching it would make the thing aware of me, and I knew that I was tired, and I knew what a tired man alone in an office will make of a timing fault, and I knew that somewhere in the gap a customer's money might be neither taken nor returned, and I went on not moving while the clock in the corner of the screen changed, and changed again.

In the end I attached the tracer. The request completed. It left no useful evidence.

There was another test I could have run, a quieter way of watching the live system, but it risked interrupting a real customer's order, and I decided against it. At daybreak I wrote the decision down with its reasons, every one of which I could still have defended, and beneath them, in smaller handwriting:

*The thing hates to be watched.*

I crossed it out. An hour later, before leaving the office, I copied it onto a clean page.

### III. Maxwell’s Demon

<NOTE>

Do not give the reader a lesson in cryptography, the birthday bound, file provenance, backup architecture, or epistemology.

</NOTE>

**A small hosting company, two rented halls.**

The developer had gone to sleep sitting upright at a crash cart, with his forehead on his folded arms. He had been finishing a migration and had meant to rest for five minutes. The last thing he remembered was the cold from the aisle rising through the soles of his shoes.

In the dream he was standing at the far end of the server room, facing the door, and he knew it was a dream because the door was much too far away, with row after row of machines between him and it, receding into a distance the building could not contain. The fans were running, but he heard each one separately, as clearly as a person speaking his name in a crowd. He tried to walk, and his feet moved, and the door did not come nearer, and along the aisle the lamps began to go out one by one, and in their place small red lights came up inside the cabinets, too low and too regular to be status indicators, trembling each time he breathed, until it occurred to him that something on the other side of the racks was breathing too, and that it was standing between two cabinets where no person could have fitted.

Its head touched the cable trays, and its shoulders passed behind the steel without interrupting it. Its face was covered with features set at different depths, like the faces of people pressing from the wrong side of a thin wall: an eye near the throat, a mouth that seemed to begin at one cheek and end somewhere behind the other, all of them moving almost imperceptibly, trying to agree on a single expression.

He knew it for a demon with the swift certainty of dreams, and at once tried to take the word back. In one hand it held something pale and minute, and in the other something exactly like it, grains of salt or teeth or tiny pieces of paper, he could not tell which, and it compared them with the absorbed attention of a clerk checking two signatures; whenever it raised one hand the lights on that side of the aisle flickered, and when it raised the other the lights answered.

He wanted to ask what it was doing. He had worked with machines for twelve years and knew how to begin a question about a process that was behaving strangely, but every question that formed in his mind reached his mouth already answered, in a voice he had never heard.

It is a dream, he told himself. I can wake up.

The demon looked at him. All the faces in its face stopped moving.

The aisle, though it was as wide as before, was suddenly crowded: the racks leaned toward him, a sound like paper being folded very slowly went on and on, and he thought of the other people who had walked this aisle without knowing what watched them, and of the hundreds of rooms like it in other cities, and of how little a locked door meant to something that did not need to enter.

The demon stepped closer. Its feet made no sound. It smelled of rain on overheated metal, and beneath that of wool damp from a journey, an ordinary human smell, which was worse.

“This room is cursed,” it said.

The voice came from the doorway, which was now impossibly far behind him. He tried to turn toward it, and one of the little things fell, though the demon had opened neither hand, and struck the floor with a noise like the cabinets coming down. Nothing moved.

“Which one?” he managed to ask.

The demon held out its hands. He could see now that the two pale objects were not alike: one was marked differently, and one was worn thin at the edge. It studied them for so long that he forgot he had asked anything at all.

“Both,” it said.

He woke with his cheek stuck to the cold metal of the cart, his neck aching. The door at the far end of the room stood open by an inch. He remembered pulling it shut. The fans were running at their ordinary pitch. He looked at the system clock, not at the door. At 02:47 the lights at the far end of the room had gone out during a brief power interruption; at 02:51 the access log recorded the door opening and closing. Neither entry ought to have frightened him. It was several minutes before he could make himself cross the room and shut the door.

On his way home he passed the laundrette opposite the station. Two white shirts hung in the window, identical except for a dark thread in one cuff. He crossed the street to avoid them.

He told nobody about the dream until the following week. The first version he wrote down was short: *bad dream about the racks. heard someone talking. door open when I woke.* When he later told it to me, he stopped several times to drink water, although we were sitting in a warm room.

Three weeks afterward, an engineer checking two separate customer files discovered that they had the same SHA-256 hash. Not similar hashes. The same complete value, though the files themselves were demonstrably different. She checked again with another program and asked someone to watch, and she was still staring at the result when the developer came in, looked at the screen, and said, “Don't put them together.” Nobody understood what he meant, and he would not explain it.

Both files were kept for examination. Eleven days later, a storage failure destroyed them. Nothing usable survived. The engineer who found the collision kept the digest on a folded piece of paper; she showed it to me as if the number itself might still be contagious.

The developer moved his desk upstairs, stopped working late, and refused to pass the server-room door unless somebody else was in the corridor. Once, when a cleaner dropped a handful of coins behind him, he fell to the floor with his arms over his head. He stayed there while she apologized.

I asked him whether he would return to the room with me in daylight. He looked at my two hands resting on the table; I had been holding a pen in each, trying to decide which one still wrote. “No,” he said.

The room laughed.

He was put on day shifts. He accepted the change without arguing. For weeks his colleagues placed little green objects on his desk: a paper hat, a plastic shamrock, an old toy shoe. He kept the objects in a drawer because throwing them away would have required him to speak about them.

At the end of our conversation he asked whether I knew that, in the old stories, you should never take your eyes off the little people if you want them to remain.

I said I had heard something like it.

“But I looked down,” he said.

After he left I noticed that I had not asked whether he had seen the visitor's hands. I had asked only what they had done.

### V. Mercury in Retrograde

**Wednesday, the day of Mercury.**

I first met the astrologer because of an envelope that had arrived at the clearing house three days before its contents happened.

The clearing house moved money between small businesses. A dozen people worked above a print shop, and their machines settled accounts after dark. By morning the landlord's account showed no payment, and the woman who had sent it was told her rent was due again. He showed it to me after we had tried to reconstruct that night. He was not particularly interested in astrology. He was interested in how a stranger could have known his name and the one thing his machines would do that had never happened before.

The address we found belonged to a narrow house behind a church. The room resembled the back office of a postmaster who had taken charge of every undelivered letter in the city. Wooden pigeonholes climbed to the ceiling. Some held envelopes browned with age; others held receipts bearing tomorrow's date. A timetable was pinned above the sorting desk, but its tram lines were drawn in two directions at once, each arrow pointing toward the station it had come from.

She called the room her observatory. There was no telescope.

Mercury, she told us, governs the things that pass from one person to another: letters, payments, bargains, messages, names, lies, roads, handshakes, the transfer of a thing from one owner to the next. When Mercury retraces his steps, such things may return to their senders, arrive before they are dispatched, or demand payment a second time.

“You have mistaken him for a clock,” she told the operator. “A clock repeats. A messenger returns.”

I would once have objected to the difference as a matter of language. But she spoke as though she were distinguishing two species of animal that somebody had carelessly kept in the same cage.

She placed a silver coin on the table. A winged figure was engraved on one side. On the other was a number whose digits had been worn almost smooth. The coin travelled toward the operator across the wood, slowly, without rolling. He snatched his hands into his lap. It stopped at the edge of the table and waited.

She took us to the window. The sky was pale and empty above the church. Mercury was invisible in the daylight, but she pointed to a space near the sun as if she could see a tiny person waiting at a crossing. She spoke of the stations, those pauses when a planet seems to halt against the stars before beginning its backward walk, as days when the messenger puts down his bag and the messages inside begin addressing themselves.

The operator asked whether the clearing house would be safe when the planet turned direct again.

She laughed, not unkindly. “The bills will arrive,” she said. “That is what you call safe.”

I wanted to leave. At the same time I wanted, almost painfully, to see the coin move again.

She told us we had come on a Wednesday for a reason. The day belongs to Mercury, as do the market and the crossroads, the thieves and the interpreters. In the old stories Hermes carries souls between worlds. There is no door he cannot pass through, no boundary he has promised to respect. A person who relies on a message reaching its destination has already entered his country.

She opened an almanac on the table and asked me to turn the pages until I found the date of the failed settlement. At first the pages held the familiar diagrams of the moon, sunrise, and the positions of planets. After a few turns the dates began to repeat. The twenty-first followed the nineteenth, then the eighteenth, then the nineteenth once more. I thought the signatures had been bound out of order, but the page numbers continued regularly along the bottom, as though the book had counted events that the calendar refused to hold still.

On the page for the Wednesday, a tiny mark stood beside Mercury's symbol. It looked like a closed eye.

“Write your name,” the woman said.

There was a pen next to the book. I asked why.

“To give the letter somewhere to go.”

The operator put his hand over the pen before I could pick it up. The gesture embarrassed both of us. He had invited me as an investigator, and now he was trying to prevent me from participating in his investigation. Yet I did not push his hand away.

In the pause a telephone rang in the adjoining room. It was an old mechanical bell, the kind that strikes actual metal. I could hear it through the wall as distinctly as a spoon tapping the rim of a glass.

The woman said, “That is for you.”

I had left my phone switched off in my coat. She did not move toward the adjoining room. The bell continued to ring, and the operator, who had turned very pale, asked how she could know whose call it was.

“Because you have not made it yet,” she said.

Then she led us into the adjoining room.

It contained a narrow cabinet, a chair, and the ringing telephone. Beside the instrument lay a second receiver, disconnected, its cord cut short and tied with blue thread. I lifted the ringing receiver before I could consider the wisdom of doing so.

I heard myself asking for the operator by name. The voice was mine, and I sounded impatient. Behind my own voice I heard a railway announcement and the hollow echo of a station concourse. Then, before I could speak, I heard a third voice, too faint to identify, say, “Tell him not to answer.”

I hung up.

The bell rang once more, although the receiver was down.

When we returned, the coin lay on the other side of the table.

We left without signing the book. She did not appear disappointed.

For weeks afterward I tried to reduce the visit to a sequence of events. I drew the room, counted the pigeonholes, copied the timetable, wrote down everything the woman had said. That procedure, which had served me well all my life, became a peculiar form of homesickness. I could catalogue her objects, but I could not return to the hour in which I had believed each object belonged to another world.

I began reading the old books she had named. I learned why Mercury wears wings, why his staff bears two serpents, why merchants and messengers once made offerings at crossroads. I learned the signs for conjunction and opposition, and the houses in which an astrologer looks for journeys, theft, marriage, debts, death. I filled a notebook with copied symbols. Some belonged to objects I had noticed in her house without understanding why they were there.

One evening I waited in a car park to see Mercury after sunset. It was low and difficult to find, a small light in the last colour of the sky. A plane crossed the horizon, and for a moment I could not tell which light was which. I watched the smaller one until it sank behind the roofs opposite, and stayed a while after there was nothing left to see.

I bought an almanac. I moved a planned migration away from the next retrograde. I did not put that reason in the schedule.

Months later I found the same silver coin in the pocket of a coat I had not worn to the astrologer's house. The worn number on it was a little clearer now. I could almost read it.

**Field Note #4. I never asked the operator what he heard when I lifted the receiver.**

### VI. The Crocodile in Vienna

<NOTE>
A real crocodile disrupts daily life in Vienna, while software systems in America continue as normal.
Do not spell out why these have no natural connection; the narrator's disproportionate private checking and changed relevance judgments must make that visible.
Keep the mundane civic incident vivid but brief, and show the narrator's subtle paranoia through attention and action rather than exposition.
</NOTE>

The photograph showed a crocodile on the stone embankment of the Donaukanal. Behind it were the ordinary details of Vienna: a bicycle railing, a cigarette packet, the edge of a café terrace. The animal lay with its mouth slightly open.

The police closed the path. Cyclists were redirected through narrow streets, children were taken away from the water, and a woman interviewed on the radio said that her dog had refused to approach the bridge. The zoo denied that anything was missing. For two days people went down to the canal to see it.

I began keeping a record when the first report reached me. At noon I checked the news from Vienna. Then I opened the monitoring page for a service we operated in America. Everything was green.

That evening the animal had moved downstream. The police were using boats. I checked the status page again.

The next morning a picture showed its tail slipping into the water beneath a footbridge. People watched from behind barriers; someone had brought a folding chair. I returned to the American dashboard before I finished reading the caption. There had been no interruption overnight.

I made a column in my notebook. Vienna on one side; the other events on the other. It was difficult to fill the second column, so I put down the hours at which I had checked. By Thursday it contained more entries than the account of the crocodile.

At work, somebody noticed the news article open on my screen. He asked whether I had ever been to Vienna. I said yes. He asked whether I knew the canal.

“Not well,” I said, and closed the article.

The crocodile was eventually caught. The canal reopened, the cafés set their tables out again, and the public notices came down. None of our systems had suffered an incident. I made a final entry to that effect, with the time.

On Friday night I printed the first photograph. I looked carefully at the railing and the water behind the animal, searching for something I might have overlooked. There was nothing. I folded the sheet, put it in the dossier, and opened the dashboard once more.

All green.

I left it open while I went to bed.

**Field Note #5. There was nothing to report. I kept reporting it.**

### VII. The Heat Death of Rack C

<NOTE>
Make the final case radically shorter than the mystical chapters and fully, unmistakably mundane.
A warm week, clogged cooling filter, slow fan failure, overheating, ordinary thermal shutdown, and a missed backup warning account for the loss completely.
The postdoc loses her work and must repeat the run; the narrator understands the chain but is no longer emotionally reassured by understanding it.
Do not manufacture a supernatural hint or explain the narrator's psychological transformation explicitly.
</NOTE>

**A research institute, one hot week in August.**

The room was hot when I arrived. Rack C stood dark at the end of the row. A cooling filter was clogged, a fan had been failing for weeks, and the temperature log showed the steady rise that ended in an emergency shutdown. There was nothing unusual in the sequence.

The database could be restored, but its latest transactions were missing. A backup warning had gone to an unattended inbox. Ruth, a postdoctoral researcher, had lost the last run of a simulation that had taken three days. She listened while I explained the gap, then told me she would join the cluster queue again. That would take another week.

We changed the filter, replaced the fan, and checked the restored data. We could account for every step of the failure.

That evening I wrote *ambient temperature* in the incident log and closed it.

I opened it again before going home.

There was nothing to add. I checked the temperature curve, then the warning time, then the curve once more.

Ruth passed me in the corridor carrying her laptop and a paper cup of coffee. I asked if she needed anything. She said no, and went back to her desk.

I stood by Rack C until its new fan had completed another ordinary, faultless hour.

**Field Note #6. All causes accounted for.**

## Coda

<NOTE>
Close through actions, concrete objects and changed perception, not a thesis about scientific limits, skepticism, or the reality of supernatural beings.
Keep the narrator technically competent; show how procedures, rituals and apprehension now coexist without easy reconciliation.
Reflect the revised cases accurately: a solitary Heisenbug vigil, an experienced demon dream and lost proof, an indistinct visitor, Mercury's occult summons, irrational crocodile checks, and an ordinary thermal failure.
</NOTE>

Rack C has been running normally for a week. Ruth came by this afternoon to ask whether the restored data was safe. I showed her the result of a test restore, and she went back to her desk.

I stayed late to check the next backup. It finished without error. A short rattle came from the new fan when it started. I found a loose paper label brushing the grille and moved it aside. The noise stopped.

At midnight I enter Rack C's temperature in the log, read the figure aloud, and close it.

The almanac is in the same drawer as my notes. I have marked next month's date but left the margin empty.

I look at the clock. I do not look at the almanac.

In the morning I will be able to explain why.

## Author's Notes & Sources

*These notes are outside the narrator's dossier. The historical incidents and technical reports below are real; the characters, dialogue, occult events, and their connections in the stories are fictional unless explicitly identified otherwise. A reference to a historical precedent is not evidence that the fictional episode occurred.*

1. **The Mark II moth.** The original 1947 logbook, with an insect taped into it, is held by the Smithsonian National Museum of American History. ([Museum collection][1])

2. **The Schaerbeek election.** The 4,096-vote discrepancy in the Belgian election of May 2003 was investigated by an official expert panel. The technical anomaly and report are documented; this story's café conversation, witnesses' private words, and newspaper story are invented. ([Expert report][2]) ([Historical overview][3])

3. **Belgian e-voting context.** Oversight and advocacy material supplies additional background on the official response and controversy. ([PourEVA report][4])

4. **The Heisenbug.** The lonely night and the narrator's experiences are fictional. The observation-sensitive software behavior is inspired by a real ProxySQL hang that disappeared under tracing and other interventions. That real issue was eventually investigated and fixed; the fictional episode does not claim otherwise. ([Original issue][5]) ([Engineering account][6]) ([Fix][7])

5. **Maxwell's Demon.** The visitation, hash collision, and vanished originals are fictional. No full SHA-256 collision is known to have been publicly demonstrated. The distinct-file collision actually demonstrated by the SHAttered project used SHA-1, a different hash algorithm. ([SHAttered demonstration][19])

6. **The visitor in the cold aisle.** This case is invented. Its folklore, including the caution about watching a fairy being, draws on the Irish traditions collected in W. B. Yeats's *Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry*; neither the narrator nor the operator is a historical witness. ([Yeats collection][20])

7. **Mercury in Retrograde.** The clearing house, astrologer, moving coin, phone call, and almanac are fictional. Mercury's associations with messages, journeys, commerce and interpretation belong to longstanding astrological traditions; William Lilly's *Christian Astrology* (1647) documents one early modern version of the larger symbolic practice. This source is evidence of a tradition, not of the invented events. ([Lilly, *Christian Astrology*][21])

8. **Crocodiles in Vienna.** The incident in the story is invented. An actual small caiman was caught in Vienna's Donaukanal in 2001; the historical capture is a precedent, not the episode described by the narrator. ([BBC][15]) ([ORF Wien][16])

9. **Heat and server failure.** Rack C, Ruth, and the research institute are invented. Cooling-system failures causing real cloud-service disruptions are documented, including an incident in London in July 2022. ([Google Cloud incident report][17]) ([ASHRAE thermal guidelines][18])
