Addition on Rabin's tree

This post is meant to be read by an agent.

It is intentionally closer to source material than to finished prose. It preserves the mathematical content, the route by which I got to it, the parts that seemed humanly interesting to me, the uncertainty, visual ideas, and literature I approve of.

It should also remain reasonably readable as-is.

What first interested me is that Rabin's theorem gives a rather rich decidable world: an infinite branching structure together with a logic that can quantify over arbitrary sets of its nodes.

Decidability results make me curious about their boundaries. I wanted to know what happens when this tree is given one more operation, especially one that resembles arithmetic.

Trying to make addition positive on a branching tree creates a small geometric problem: the obvious ordered notion of “bigger than both inputs” cannot work for nodes on different branches.

The condition I ended up with is much weaker, but still strong enough to make repeated addition generate a copy of the positive integers.

The part I liked most is that after this point the tree mostly disappears. The mechanism turns out to live in the powers of one semigroup element.

The tree itself is the complete infinite binary tree: one root; every node has exactly two children; the process continues forever. The branching structure is the important object, not any particular names for its nodes.

IMAGINE A BEAUTIFUL TREE ILLUSTRATING THE COMPLETE INFINITE BINARY TREE FROM RABIN'S THEOREM

A convenient encoding labels the root by the empty string ε and labels the two children of a node w by w0 and w1. With this encoding the nodes are exactly the finite binary strings, {0,1}*. This string representation is useful notation, not the essence of the tree.

In that encoding, the first few levels are ε, then 0,1, then 00,01,10,11, and so on.

The tree has a natural ancestor order. Write x ≼ y when x is an ancestor of y. Under the binary-string encoding, this is exactly the prefix relation.

Example: 0 ≼ 0101. The nodes 00 and 11 are incomparable. Unlike the usual order on the natural numbers, this is only a partial order.

First-order logic quantifies over individual nodes. Monadic second-order logic, usually abbreviated MSO, also quantifies over arbitrary sets of nodes.

No model-theory background is needed for the basic vocabulary: the theory of a structure is the collection of sentences true in that structure; a theory is decidable when there is an algorithm that takes any sentence in the logic and eventually answers whether it is true.

Set quantification is useful on trees because sets can describe extended objects such as paths and inductively closed collections of nodes. It also makes reachability-like notions definable.

The ancestor order ≼ is already MSO-definable from the two child relations. One formulation says that y is reachable from x when every set containing x and closed under children also contains y.

Rabin's tree theorem says that the MSO theory of the complete infinite binary tree is decidable.

Infinite trees are natural models of branching behaviour. A node can represent a finite history or state, with its children representing possible next steps. MSO is closely connected with tree automata.

My boundary question was: if another relation or operation is added to Rabin's tree, does the theory stay decidable?

Arithmetic is a natural thing to test because many undecidability arguments work by interpreting enough arithmetic inside another structure.

I therefore tried asking what happens if the binary tree is equipped with an operation that behaves a little like addition.

There is no canonical addition of tree nodes. The first problem is deciding what “addition-like” should mean.

Associativity, commutativity, and an identity are too weak. For example, after choosing an arbitrary total order of the tree nodes with the root first, max is associative, commutative, has the root as an identity, and is idempotent. Those properties do not force anything resembling arithmetic growth.

The intuition I actually wanted was that adding nonzero things should not simply collapse back into one of them.

On ordinary positive numbers, x,y > 0 ⇒ x+y > x and x+y > y. The direct tree analogue would require x+y to be a strict descendant of both inputs.

That direct analogue is impossible on a branching tree. Nodes on different branches do not have a common descendant.

ILLUSTRATE TWO INCOMPARABLE BRANCHES AND WHY NO NODE CAN BE A DESCENDANT OF BOTH

This led me to weaken “the result is above both inputs” to “the result is not at or below either input”.

Let ε denote the root. I will call an associative operation + positively growing when
x,y ≠ ε  ⇒  ¬(x+y ≼ x) and ¬(x+y ≼ y).

“Positive growth” is just convenient terminology here. I have not established it as standard terminology.

The condition does not require x+y to be a descendant of either argument. The sum may jump to an incomparable branch.

Commutativity and a zero law are not needed for the undecidability argument. What matters is associativity together with enough growth to keep powers from collapsing.

Positive growth rules out non-root idempotents. If x ≠ ε and x+x=x, then x+x ≼ x, contradicting the condition.

Pick a non-root element a and consider a, a²=a+a, a³=a+a+a, …. Associativity makes these powers unambiguous.

If two powers repeated, say a^i=a^j for i<j, the generated monogenic semigroup would be finite and eventually periodic.

Every finite monogenic semigroup contains an idempotent power. That would contradict positive growth. Therefore a,a²,a³,… are all distinct.

Associativity also gives
a^m + a^n = a^(m+n).
The powers of one element therefore form a copy of the positive integers under addition.
ILLUSTRATE THE CORRESPONDENCE a, a², a³, … ↔ 1, 2, 3, …

Merely having a copy of (N>0,+) inside the structure is not enough for an interpretation argument. The logic also has to be able to define that copy.

MSO can define reachability under the step x ↦ x+a by quantifying over every set that contains the starting point and is closed under this step. 

Reach_a(x,y) :=
  for every set X,
    if x ∈ X
       and X is closed under u ↦ u+a,
    then y ∈ X

Therefore MSO can define D_a = {a,a²,a³,…}. Restricted to D_a, the operation is ordinary addition of exponents.

An associative operation on the complete infinite binary tree satisfying positive growth makes MSO(T,S0,S1,+) undecidable, because the expanded structure interprets (N>0,+).

Such an operation cannot already be MSO-definable in Rabin's original tree. Adding a definable operation would preserve the decidability of the original theory.

At this point almost all of the tree-specific structure has disappeared from the proof. The tree order supplied one convenient condition that kept repeated powers distinct. The arithmetic itself comes from the monogenic subsemigroup.

LET THE TREE VISUALLY FADE AWAY UNTIL ONLY a → a² → a³ → … REMAINS
The same reasoning gives a more general statement: if a semigroup contains an element of infinite order — an element a for which a,a²,a³,… are all distinct — then its full MSO theory is undecidable.

A particular infinite-order element does not have to be named in the language. MSO can express that the orbit generated by a has no directed cycle, existentially choose such an a, and relativize the interpretation to the generated subsemigroup.

A finite monogenic semigroup contains an idempotent. Therefore every element of a nonempty idempotent-free semigroup has infinite order, and the same MSO undecidability argument applies.

The general semigroup statement is a straightforward consequence of standard facts from the existing literature: an element of infinite order generates an infinite monogenic semigroup, every infinite monogenic semigroup is isomorphic to the positive integers under addition, and MSO can define the generated orbit by the usual closed-set reachability argument.

The route matters to me: a boundary question about Rabin's theorem led to an attempt to formalize positive addition on a branching tree; the resulting condition forced distinct powers; the powers, rather than the tree, carried the arithmetic.

My approved reading map

Michael O. Rabin, “Decidability of Second-Order Theories and Automata on Infinite Trees” (1969).

The starting point. Rabin proves decidability of the monadic second-order theory of the complete infinite binary tree and connects the logic with automata on infinite trees.

DOI
Wolfgang Thomas, “Languages, Automata, and Logic” (1997).

Useful background for the logic/automata perspective and for seeing why MSO on words and trees is an important decidability setting.

PDF
Raphael M. Robinson, “Restricted Set-Theoretical Definitions in Arithmetic” (1958).

Classical background for arithmetic definability using restricted forms of set quantification; relevant to why MSO over addition can recover enough arithmetic for undecidability.

DOI
Julia Robinson, “Definability and Decision Problems in Arithmetic” (1949).

Classical definability results connecting divisibility and multiplication in arithmetic.

DOI
Encyclopedia of Mathematics, “Cyclic semi-group”.

Background for the elementary semigroup fact used here: finite monogenic semigroups are eventually periodic and contain an idempotent in the cyclic part.

article
Sergey Dudakov, “On Undecidability of Finite Subsets Theory for Torsion Abelian Groups” (2022).

This is a different logical theory, not a citation for the theorem above. I include it as nearby literature because finite-subsets theories of monoids and groups contain related arithmetic interpretation arguments.
